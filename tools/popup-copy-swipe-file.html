Popup copy swipe file: 24 reusable patterns

By Shailu, founder of PopupLoom Published 16 November 2026 Last updated 16 November 2026 Copy patterns, not conversion claims

This is a starting file, not a list of winners. Each pattern gives you a headline, a body, a button and a line of fine print for a specific popup job. Replace every bracketed placeholder with something true about your offer, then test it on your own traffic.

PopupLoom has not run an A/B test on every pattern and does not claim a conversion rate for any of them. The notes explain why the structure is put together that way — one job, one offer, one action — not how many signups it will produce. If you see a number in a swipe file that promises a lift, ask what traffic it came from, how long it ran and whether the offer was the same.

The one rule that matters most: do not invent a fact to make the copy sound stronger. A real deadline, a real stock count, a real price and a real testimonial are persuasive. A fake countdown, a made-up “only 2 left” message or a review you cannot substantiate is a liability, not a tactic.

The patterns below are deliberately plain. Plain copy is easier to edit, easier to translate and easier to defend when a customer asks what you meant.

How to use this file

  1. Pick the job first. A welcome offer, a restock alert and a feedback prompt are different jobs. Do not start with a design and then look for something to say.
  2. Replace every placeholder. The offer, code, date, product, price, link and privacy notice must be real. Delete any line you cannot fill truthfully.
  3. Cut the copy in half. A popup is an interruption. If a sentence does not help the visitor decide, remove it.
  4. Check the close control. The visitor must be able to close the popup without submitting an email address or accepting an offer.
  5. Test one change at a time. A new headline, a new offer and a new trigger at once tells you nothing about which one mattered.

The anatomy of a popup that respects the visitor

  • One job. Subscribe, buy, read, register or answer. If the popup asks for two unrelated actions, it is two popups fighting for one screen.
  • One offer. The headline should name the reward or the reason to act. “Subscribe to our newsletter” is a mechanism; “Get the 12-point launch checklist” is an offer.
  • A specific action. The button repeats the verb: “Send the checklist”, “Shop with CODE”, “Save my seat”. “Submit” tells the visitor nothing.
  • Honest urgency. A date, a time zone, a stock limit or a shipping cutoff is useful when it is real. If the deadline moves every night, it is not a deadline.
  • A visible exit. Close, “No thanks” or “Keep reading” must do what it says. A decline link that opens the same offer is a dark pattern.
  • Fine print that answers the next question. Exclusions, one-use limits, delivery estimates and what happens to an email address belong in the popup, not only on a page the visitor never opens.

Welcome and first order

1. First-order welcome with a real code

Job: turn a first-time visitor into a first order. Use when: you have a real discount, a real code and a real expiry date.

Headline: Your first order, [PERCENT]% off
Body: Use [CODE] at checkout. One use per customer. Valid until [DATE].
Button: Shop with [CODE]
Fine print: [PERCENT]% off one order. Excludes [EXCLUSIONS]. Cannot be combined with other offers. See [TERMS LINK].

Why the structure works: the headline names the reward, the body gives the mechanics, the button repeats the code and the fine print states the limits. Nothing depends on the visitor opening another page to understand the offer.

2. New-arrival early access

Job: capture an email before a launch. Use when: the collection and the date are already scheduled.

Headline: First look: [COLLECTION]
Body: New pieces go live [DATE]. Get the early-access link before the public launch.
Button: Send me the early link
Fine print: By subscribing you agree to [PRIVACY NOTICE]. You can unsubscribe at any time.

Why the structure works: the offer is access, not a discount. That only works if the early link actually arrives before the public launch and the date is real.

3. Newsletter welcome without a discount

Job: build a list when you have no incentive to give away. Use when: the newsletter has a clear audience and a predictable cadence.

Headline: One useful email, [CADENCE]
Body: [WHAT THEY GET] — written by [WHO], not a daily sales blast. Read the latest issue before you decide.
Button: Get the next issue
Fine print: [CADENCE]. Unsubscribe in one click. [PRIVACY NOTICE].

Why the structure works: it answers the two questions a cautious visitor has — what will arrive, and how often. The link to a real issue lets them judge the quality before subscribing.

Email and lead magnets

4. Checklist lead magnet

Job: capture an email in exchange for a practical asset. Use when: the checklist exists and can be delivered automatically.

Headline: The [N]-point [TOPIC] checklist
Body: A printable checklist for [SPECIFIC TASK]. No account needed — enter your email and we will send the link.
Button: Send the checklist
Fine print: One email with the download and [CADENCE] follow-ups. Unsubscribe at any time. [PRIVACY NOTICE].

Why the structure works: the asset is specific, the effort is stated and the follow-up is disclosed. “No account needed” removes a step the visitor may be dreading.

5. Template pack

Job: capture an email for a reusable design asset. Use when: the pack is genuinely usable and the licence is clear.

Headline: [N] [FORMAT] templates for [AUDIENCE]
Body: Copy the structure, replace the placeholders and publish. The pack includes [FORMAT 1], [FORMAT 2] and [FORMAT 3].
Button: Get the template pack
Fine print: Delivered by email as [FILE TYPE]. [LICENCE TERMS].

Why the structure works: it tells the visitor what they can do with the pack, not just what is inside it. The licence line prevents a surprise when they try to use it commercially.

6. Webinar registration

Job: register a qualified attendee. Use when: the speaker, date, time zone and topic are confirmed.

Headline: Live on [DATE]: [TOPIC]
Body: [SPEAKER] walks through [SPECIFIC OUTCOME] in [DURATION]. Bring one question for the Q&A.
Button: Save my seat
Fine print: [DATE] at [TIME] [TIMEZONE]. Replay sent to registrants. [PRIVACY NOTICE].

Why the structure works: a live event has a hard deadline, so the date does the persuasive work. Naming the outcome is more useful than listing the speaker's entire biography.

Exit intent

7. Cart-page reminder (written by the owner)

Job: bring a visitor back to the cart. Use when: the platform saves the cart and you can name the product truthfully.

Headline: Still deciding on [PRODUCT]?
Body: Your [PRODUCT] is waiting in the cart. [SHIPPING / RETURNS / STOCK FACT].
Button: Return to cart
Fine print: [OFFER OR NO OFFER]. [TERMS LINK].

Why the structure works: it repeats the product the visitor was already looking at. Important: PopupLoom does not read cart contents or customer accounts. Write the product name yourself or use a generic line, and do not claim the cart is saved if the platform does not save it.

8. Content-page exit offer

Job: offer a resource to a reader who is leaving. Use when: the resource is relevant to the page they are on.

Headline: Before you go: [RESOURCE]
Body: [ONE-SENTENCE VALUE]. It is free and takes [TIME] to read.
Button: Get [RESOURCE]
Fine print: [PRIVACY NOTICE]. No purchase required.

Why the structure works: the resource, the effort and the cost are all explicit. A reader who is not interested can close the popup without being tricked into a sales call.

9. Exit feedback

Job: learn why the page did not answer the visitor's question. Use when: you will actually read and act on the answers.

Headline: Did this page answer your question?
Body: One click helps us fix the gaps. No email required.
Button: Yes, it answered it
Secondary: Not quite — tell us what is missing
Fine print: Answers are anonymous unless you add an email.

Why the structure works: it asks one question and offers two honest paths. Both buttons must work; a “Not quite” button that secretly submits a sales form is worse than no feedback popup at all.

Announcements and operations

10. Shipping cutoff

Job: set a delivery expectation before a busy period. Use when: the cutoff and the carrier window are confirmed.

Headline: Order by [DATE/TIME] for [DELIVERY WINDOW]
Body: Orders placed after the cutoff ship on [NEXT SHIP DATE]. [REGION] delivery only.
Button: Shop in-stock items
Fine print: Delivery estimates are not guarantees. [CARRIER / REGION DETAILS].

Why the structure works: it gives the visitor a decision they can make now. The fine print separates an estimate from a promise, which matters when a carrier delay is outside your control.

11. New hours or service area

Job: tell returning visitors what changed. Use when: the change is permanent or starts on a known date.

Headline: We are open [NEW HOURS]
Body: Starting [DATE], [LOCATION/SERVICE] runs [HOURS]. [WHAT CHANGES FOR CUSTOMERS].
Button: See the full schedule
Fine print: Holiday hours may differ. [CONTACT LINK].

Why the structure works: it leads with the change, not with an apology. The button points to the durable schedule instead of forcing the popup to contain every exception.

12. Maintenance notice

Job: stop customers from retrying a broken action. Use when: a service is genuinely down and you have a status update.

Headline: [SERVICE] is down for maintenance
Body: We are fixing [SPECIFIC ISSUE]. Expected back by [TIME] [TIMEZONE]. Orders already placed are unaffected.
Button: Read the status update
Fine print: Updates posted at [STATUS LINK]. No need to email us unless [CONDITION].

Why the structure works: it answers the three questions a customer has during an outage: what broke, when it will be back and whether their existing order is safe. Only publish the expected time if you have a real basis for it.

Waitlists and restocks

13. Back-in-stock alert

Job: capture demand for an unavailable product. Use when: you can actually send the restock message.

Headline: [PRODUCT] is out of stock
Body: Leave your email and we will send one message when it is back. No daily reminders.
Button: Notify me
Fine print: One restock email. No purchase obligation. [PRIVACY NOTICE].

Why the structure works: it sets the frequency expectation explicitly. If you cannot send the message, do not collect the address; an unfulfilled restock list is a support queue, not a lead magnet.

14. New-city waitlist

Job: measure demand before a local launch. Use when: the city is under consideration and the date is an estimate.

Headline: [CITY], we are coming
Body: Join the launch list for [SERVICE] in [CITY]. We will email the date and the first available slots.
Button: Join the [CITY] list
Fine print: Launch date is an estimate. [PRIVACY NOTICE].

Why the structure works: it is honest about the uncertainty. “We are coming” is a claim; pairing it with “launch date is an estimate” keeps the promise defensible.

15. Product launch waitlist

Job: build an audience before a product is purchasable. Use when: the launch date and the early-access benefit are real.

Headline: [PRODUCT] launches [DATE]
Body: Get the launch link and [EARLY ACCESS DETAIL] before the public announcement.
Button: Put me on the list
Fine print: One launch email. [PRIVACY NOTICE]. No payment is taken now.

Why the structure works: it removes the fear of an accidental charge. The early-access detail must be something you can actually deliver, such as a 24-hour head start or a limited quantity.

Reviews and community

16. Review request

Job: collect a review from a real customer. Use when: the purchase is complete and the review policy is published.

Headline: How was [PRODUCT]?
Body: A two-minute review helps the next customer choose. We read every response.
Button: Leave a review
Fine print: Reviews are published with your display name. [REVIEW POLICY LINK].

Why the structure works: it explains who benefits from the review and how it will be published. Do not offer an undisclosed incentive, edit a negative review or ask only happy customers; those practices undermine the review's value and may breach endorsement rules.

17. Case-study invitation

Job: find a customer willing to be featured. Use when: you will ask for approval before publishing anything.

Headline: Would you share how you use [PRODUCT]?
Body: We are looking for [ROLE] to feature in a short case study. We will ask before publishing anything.
Button: Tell us about your setup
Fine print: Participation is optional. [PRIVACY NOTICE]. No payment is implied.

Why the structure works: it states the ask, the audience and the approval step. “No payment is implied” prevents a misunderstanding about compensation.

18. Community invitation

Job: invite the right person into a community. Use when: the community is active and the rules are visible.

Headline: Join [COMMUNITY] for [AUDIENCE]
Body: [WHAT HAPPENS THERE] — [CADENCE] and [RULES]. Read the pinned post before you introduce yourself.
Button: Open the community
Fine print: [PLATFORM] account required. [COMMUNITY RULES LINK].

Why the structure works: it describes the room before asking someone to enter it. A community invite that promises “networking” without saying who is there or how often they meet attracts the wrong members.

Seasonal and events

19. Black Friday or Cyber Monday

Job: announce a dated sale. Use when: the dates, exclusions and stock position are confirmed.

Headline: [SALE NAME]: [OFFER] until [DATE]
Body: Use [CODE] at checkout. [WHAT IS INCLUDED]. [WHAT IS EXCLUDED].
Button: Shop the sale
Fine print: Ends [DATE] at [TIME] [TIMEZONE]. While stocks last. Cannot be combined with other offers. [TERMS LINK].

Why the structure works: the deadline is in the headline and repeated with a time zone in the fine print. If the sale is extended later, update the page and the popup; do not quietly keep the old countdown running.

20. Holiday shipping deadline

Job: prevent a late order that will miss the holiday. Use when: the carrier's last-ship date is known.

Headline: Last order date for [HOLIDAY] delivery
Body: Order by [DATE/TIME] with [SHIPPING METHOD]. After that, choose [ALTERNATIVE].
Button: See shipping options
Fine print: Delivery estimates depend on the carrier. [REGION] only. [TERMS LINK].

Why the structure works: it gives the visitor a fallback instead of a dead end. The alternative could be a digital gift card, store pickup or a later delivery window.

21. Local event or market

Job: drive attendance to an in-person event. Use when: the venue, time and entry terms are confirmed.

Headline: Meet us at [EVENT] on [DATE]
Body: [LOCATION], [TIME]. [WHAT VISITORS CAN DO]. [TICKET / FREE ENTRY DETAIL].
Button: Get the event details
Fine print: [WEATHER / CANCELLATION POLICY]. [ORGANISER LINK].

Why the structure works: it answers the practical questions — where, when, what happens and whether it costs anything — before asking for a commitment.

Feedback and responsible exits

22. One-question feedback

Job: find the friction on a page or in a flow. Use when: you can act on the answer and the options are mutually exclusive.

Headline: What stopped you from [ACTION]?
Body: Pick the closest answer. It takes one click and helps us improve this page.
Options: [PRICE] / [CHOICE] / [TRUST] / [SOMETHING ELSE]
Fine print: No email required. Answers are anonymous unless you add one.

Why the structure works: it names the action the visitor abandoned and gives a short, closed set of reasons. “Something else” needs a follow-up field; without one, it collects nothing useful.

23. Beta invitation

Job: recruit testers for an unfinished feature. Use when: you can support the testers and the limits are real.

Headline: Try [FEATURE] before it is public
Body: We are testing [WHAT IT DOES] with a small group. Expect rough edges and a feedback form.
Button: Request beta access
Fine print: Access is limited and not guaranteed. [PRIVACY NOTICE]. [SUPPORT LINK].

Why the structure works: it sets the expectation of rough edges before the visitor invests time. “Access is limited” is only honest if there is a real limit and a real selection process.

24. Responsible exit

Job: let a disinterested visitor leave without friction. Use when: you want a second chance without holding the page hostage.

Headline: Not interested? That is fine.
Body: Close this window and keep reading. If you want [RESOURCE], the link is below.
Button: Close
Secondary: Send me [RESOURCE]
Fine print: No email is required to close. [PRIVACY NOTICE].

Why the structure works: the decline is the primary action and it actually closes the popup. The optional resource stays available without pretending the visitor chose it.

What not to write

These patterns are common because they work on attention, not because they are honest. Avoid them unless the underlying fact is true and documented:

  • “Only 2 left!” when the number is not connected to inventory or when the “2” never changes.
  • “Sale ends tonight” with a timer that resets on the next visit.
  • “You have won” when there is no prize, no draw and no terms.
  • A “No thanks” link that opens the same offer or submits a form.
  • A hidden or tiny close button that makes the popup hard to dismiss, especially on mobile.
  • A pre-checked consent box or a sentence that treats silence as marketing permission.
  • Testimonials or review counts you cannot substantiate, or a review you edited to remove a real complaint.
  • “Guaranteed results” for a service whose outcome depends on the customer's traffic, offer or market.
  • Health, financial or legal claims that need evidence you do not have.
  • A fake countdown on a page that is not actually for sale yet.

In the United States, the FTC's truth-in-advertising guidance is a useful baseline: ads must be truthful, not misleading and substantiated. Other countries have their own consumer-protection rules. This page is not legal advice.

How to compare two versions without fooling yourself

PopupLoom does not include A/B testing. If you want to compare two patterns, you can run them sequentially on the same page, or use a page-level split test in your own stack. Either way, the comparison is only as good as the discipline around it.

Make the comparison fair

  • Change one thing: the headline, the offer, the trigger or the button. Not all four.
  • Keep the traffic source, page and audience the same.
  • Run each version for a full business cycle, not a single busy afternoon.
  • Record the number of displays and submissions, not just the percentage.
  • Decide the stopping point before you start.

Be honest about the limits

  • A small sample can swing wildly. A 1% difference on 200 displays is noise.
  • Seasonality, campaigns and press coverage can change the audience between periods.
  • A higher submit rate is not automatically better if the leads are lower quality.
  • Repeating a test until it “wins” is not a test; it is a search for a flattering result.
  • If you cannot measure it, do not claim it in public.

Consent and privacy are part of the copy

A popup that collects an email address is a data-collection point. The copy should say what the visitor gets, how often they will hear from you and where the privacy notice is. Do not hide the unsubscribe path, do not pre-check a consent box and do not treat a closed popup as consent.

The rules depend on where your visitors live and what you do with the data. If you are unsure, ask a qualified adviser. The PopupLoom privacy page explains what the product stores; it is not a template for your own privacy notice.

Common questions

Are these popup copy examples tested winners?

No. They are structural patterns, not tested templates. PopupLoom has not run an A/B test on every pattern and does not claim a conversion rate for any of them. Replace the placeholders with true facts and test on your own traffic.

Can I use these patterns on any website?

The copy is generic and can be adapted to any site that allows a popup. Check the platform's own rules, the offer terms and the privacy and consent requirements that apply to your business before publishing.

Do I need to change the bracketed placeholders?

Yes. Every bracketed field is a placeholder, including the offer, code, date, product, link and privacy notice. Publishing a placeholder as if it were a real fact is misleading.

Does PopupLoom A/B test these patterns?

No. PopupLoom does not provide built-in A/B testing. You can compare two periods sequentially or run your own page-level split test outside the product, but you are responsible for the measurement.

Can I use fake urgency or a countdown that resets?

No. A countdown or scarcity claim should reflect a real deadline or a real stock limit. A timer that resets, or a low-stock message that is not connected to inventory, can mislead visitors and may breach consumer-protection rules.

Should every popup ask for an email address?

No. An announcement, a shipping notice or a feedback prompt may need no email at all. Ask only for the information the stated job requires, and explain what happens to it.

How many words should popup copy have?

As few as the offer needs. A discount or announcement can fit in a headline, one sentence, a button and a line of fine print. A lead magnet may need a short explanation of what the visitor gets and how it arrives.

Is this swipe file legal advice?

No. It is copywriting guidance. Consumer-protection, advertising, privacy and consent rules depend on your jurisdiction and your business. Get qualified advice for your situation.
