How to choose a free popup builder in 2026: nine checks

By Shailu, founder of PopupLoom Published 14 November 2026 Last updated 16 November 2026 Checklist reviewed 16 November 2026

This is a checklist, not a ranking. Ranked lists of popup builders go stale in weeks: plans change, trials end, prices move. The criteria below do not. Apply them to any shortlist — including PopupLoom — and you get an answer that survives the next pricing update.

No vendor is named on this page. That is deliberate: the checks are about the shape of an offer, not the logo on it. Every check ends with an honest note on where PopupLoom stands, including the four checks it fails.

Most “best free” lists answer the wrong question. They compare feature counts at a point in time, which is easy to write and easy to game. They rarely answer the question that decides whether a free plan is usable in six months: what does this plan count, and what happens when the count runs out?

The nine checks below are ordered by how often they cause a switch. The first three are about limits, the next three about features you might assume are included, and the last three about what leaving costs.

1. The metered unit

The check that decides how fast everything else runs out.

A free plan is defined by what it counts. Pageviews follow page loads, so one reader on five pages consumes five units. Unique visitors count each person once, which is friendlier to long sessions but resets on a schedule. Impressions count popup appearances, which is the closest match to the thing you are actually buying.

Find the unit on the plan card before you find the number. A “5,000” allowance means very different things in each of the three units. If the card lists a unit and your analytics reports another, convert before comparing.

PopupLoom: no metered unit for popups. Hosting and API rate limits exist but are not a plan boundary.

2. The reset

Monthly is a recurring cliff; one-time is a trial.

“Per month” tells you the plan can work indefinitely for a small site and will bind abruptly for a growing one. “One-time” tells you it ends by design. Some trials also move the account onto a paid plan when the credit runs out, which is a different product from a free tier.

For a popup you intend to leave installed for a year, a recurring allowance is more useful than a large one-off credit. Ask what happens at the reset: does the counter carry over, pause, or switch the popup off?

PopupLoom: no counter, so no reset and no at-limit behaviour to read.

3. Campaign and domain caps

Often binds before traffic does.

A plan can allow 50,000 pageviews and still let you publish only one popup. That is fine for a newsletter prompt and useless for a store running a welcome offer, a cart reminder and a seasonal banner at once. Domain caps work the same way for anyone with a second brand or a client site.

Count the campaigns you need live at the same time, not the designs you want to build. Then count the domains you actually operate. Either number can make a generous traffic allowance irrelevant.

PopupLoom: no live-campaign cap and no domain cap. Each saved widget has its own configuration, inbox and snippet.

4. Feature gates

The free plan can be free and still wrong.

Exit intent, scroll triggers, analytics, split testing, advanced targeting and scheduling are the features most often held back. A plan without them may be enough for a page-load welcome popup and not enough for anything else. Read the plan comparison line by line rather than the marketing page.

Be precise about what you need. If your only campaign is a discount code on page load, a paid trigger list is irrelevant. If you are trying to recover abandoning visitors, exit intent is the whole product.

PopupLoom: six triggers included — page load, timer, scroll depth, scroll past an element, click on a selector, and exit intent on desktop. No A/B testing, no analytics dashboard, no AI. Exit intent does not fire on touch devices.

5. Branding

A credit line is a cost, even when the plan is free.

Some free plans place a vendor credit on the popup or in its corner. That is a fair trade for a personal project and a bad one for a paid brand. Check the free card and the published screenshots rather than trusting a feature table.

PopupLoom: no credit line required by the product. The demo preview is labelled as a demo, which is different from a vendor badge.

6. Platform and install

A tool that cannot run on your site is not cheap at any price.

Plugins only work on their platform. Apps only work inside their store. Scripts work wherever the owner can add HTML or JavaScript, which is most sites but not all: some page builders sandbox third-party widgets in an iframe, and some checkout flows block custom code.

Decide the install path before the feature list. Then test it on the published page, not the editor preview.

PopupLoom: two lines before the closing body tag, hosted configuration, no plugin. Not suitable where custom scripts are blocked.

7. Data and export

You find out what leaving costs on the day you leave.

Ask where responses are stored and how you get them out. A self-hosted plugin keeps them in your database, under your backups. A hosted product keeps them in its system and should offer CSV export or an API. Neither is automatically better; the mistake is not knowing.

Export a test row before you collect real leads. If the export needs a support ticket during a trial, it will need one during a migration.

PopupLoom: responses are stored against the saved widget in the lead inbox. CSV export is included, and an optional signed webhook posts each submission to your own server with retries and a delivery log.

8. The paid cliff

Price the tier you will need, not the tier you are on.

Free tiers are priced to end. The useful number is what the next tier costs at roughly twice your current traffic, not the headline monthly figure. If the plan jumps from a small allowance to a large one, a site in the middle pays for capacity it cannot use.

Write down three numbers: the allowance you have now, the allowance you expect in six months, and the annual price of the tier that covers the second. That is the real cost of choosing the plan.

PopupLoom: no paid tier, so no cliff. The trade-off is a smaller feature set, not a future invoice.

9. Support and longevity

Free plans are still software you have to live with.

Check who answers support and whether the product has shipped recently. A changelog, a public repository or a real support address is evidence of maintenance. A free plan from a product that has not shipped in a year is a risk you are taking without a contract.

PopupLoom: a small product maintained by its founder, with email support and no uptime SLA. That is a real limitation for anyone who needs a vendor on call.

How to score a shortlist without inventing a ranking

Once the nine checks are done, most shortlists have two or three tools left. A simple score is more useful than a gut feeling, provided you keep it honest about what it measures.

Give each tool a score of 0 to 2 on the three checks that can disqualify it: the metered unit against your traffic, the feature gates for the campaign you actually plan, and the export path. Then use the remaining six checks as tie-breakers rather than points. This avoids the usual distortion where a tool wins because it has more templates and loses on the limit that will bind in eight weeks.

The scoring sheet also makes the trade-off visible to whoever else signs off on the purchase. “We chose this one because it scored 6 out of 6 on the three checks that bind” is a more useful sentence than “it looked the nicest”.

Where PopupLoom fails its own checklist

The point of a checklist is that it can disqualify the product publishing it. Four checks are worth stating plainly:

  • It fails check 4 on testing and analytics. There is no A/B engine and no dashboard. You can compare versions by running them at different times, which is weaker than a real split test.
  • It fails check 7 for self-hosting. Data is hosted, not in your database. Export and webhook make it portable, but portable is not the same as local.
  • It fails check 9 for enterprise support. No SLA, no account manager, no phone line.
  • It is weaker on mobile exit intent. Exit intent is desktop only, so phone visitors need a timer, scroll or click trigger instead.

If those matter, choose a product that passes them. The checklist is more useful as a filter than as a scoreboard.

Five red flags on any pricing page

  1. Two different limits for the same thing. A plan card and a feature table that disagree mean the page is not maintained. Ask support, in writing.
  2. No at-limit behaviour. If the page does not say whether the popup stops or the account is billed, assume neither until confirmed.
  3. “Unlimited” without a rate-limit note. Every hosted service has limits. The honest ones say what they are.
  4. Annual pricing shown as a monthly equivalent. Divide by twelve and check the cancellation terms; the monthly figure is not what you pay.
  5. No export path. A contacts product without export is a lock-in product.

The ten-minute test

Once a shortlist is down to two or three tools, test the free tier rather than reading about it:

  1. Install on one low-traffic page.
  2. Build one popup with one trigger and one frequency rule.
  3. Submit a test response and confirm it appears in the account.
  4. Export the row.
  5. Open the page in a private window on a phone and on a desktop.
  6. Turn caching on and repeat step 5.

If any step fails, the plan is not ready for a campaign. The free-tier audit guide expands this into a fuller test, and the no-limits page explains the units in more depth.

Common questions

How do I choose a free popup builder?

Start with the metered unit: pageviews, unique visitors, impressions or no meter. Then check the reset, campaign and domain caps, feature gates, branding, export path and the price of the next tier. A plan that fits today but binds in two months is not free in practice.

What is the most important check?

The metered unit, because it decides how fast everything else runs out. Pageviews track page loads, unique visitors track people once each, and impressions track popup appearances. Compare the unit to what your analytics actually reports.

Is a monthly allowance better than a trial?

A monthly allowance that resets can work indefinitely for a small site. A trial ends by design, and some trials move the account onto a paid plan automatically. For a popup you intend to leave running, a recurring allowance is more useful than a trial credit.

Which free features are usually gated?

Exit intent, scroll triggers, analytics, split testing, advanced targeting and scheduling are commonly paid-only. Confirm each one on the plan comparison rather than assuming the free tier includes it.

Do free plans force branding?

Some do. A credit line on the popup is a real cost for a paid brand, so check the free plan card and the published screenshots. PopupLoom does not require a credit line.

How do I test a free popup builder before committing?

Install it on one low-traffic page, run one popup with one trigger, submit a test response, export the row, then check the popup on a phone and with caching active. If any step fails, the plan is not ready for a campaign.

What should I check before leaving a free plan?

Confirm you can export submissions and contacts in a usable format, note the notice period, and keep the export after cancelling. No vendor is obliged to hold your historical leads indefinitely.

Does PopupLoom pass this checklist?

It passes the meter, campaign, branding and export checks, and fails the testing, analytics and AI checks. Data is hosted rather than self-hosted, and exit intent is desktop only. The checklist is designed to make those trade-offs visible.
